\chapter[Cross-Robot Evaluation]{Cross-Robot Evaluation}
\label{chapter:cross-robot-evaluation}

\begin{quote}\itshape
The objective of this chapter is to present the cross-robot evaluation of
Auto-Adapter. The chapter begins by introducing the evaluation and its
background. It then examines serial-arm manipulation, quadruped locomotion,
aerial quadrotors, and humanoid bipeds in turn. Finally, it summarises the
evidence across the evaluated configurations and states the boundaries of the
comparison.
\end{quote}

\section[Introduction]{Introduction}
\label{sec:cross-robot-introduction}

A high-level controller can reuse a task-neutral capability only when a
robot-specific driver translates it into commands and observations supported
by the target configuration. This translation differs across serial arms,
legged robots, aerial vehicles, and humanoids because their actuation,
feedback, stability requirements, and feasible control structures are not
interchangeable. Cross-robot evaluation therefore asks whether the
Auto-Adapter interface-and-driver boundary remains usable across different
robot configurations, rather than whether one implementation can be copied
between them unchanged.

This chapter covers serial-arm
manipulation, quadruped locomotion, aerial flight, and humanoid balance. Unlike
the controlled construction study in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:autoadapter-component-analysis}, these studies use
configuration-specific drivers, model settings, tasks, validation procedures,
and attempt budgets. They are therefore analysed as separate evidence blocks
rather than combined under one experimental denominator.

The analysis distinguishes driver construction and validation from downstream
task execution. It examines where the evaluated integrations succeed or fail
and whether the observed limitations arise from the generated interface,
control implementation, validation procedure, or downstream composition. The
results remain specific to the named MuJoCo configurations and do not establish
a general model ranking, a causal morphology effect, hardware behaviour, or
sim-to-real transfer.


\section[Background]{Background}

The same intended action may require different implementations because robot
joint structures, actuation methods, coordinate frames, limits, and feedback
signals differ \citep{zech2019action,pantano2022capabilitysurvey}. A capability
interface hides part of this variation from the high-level controller, but its
driver must still realise each operation using one configuration's control and
observation facilities. Experiment~3 therefore reconstructs the capability
design, validation suite, and driver independently in every cell.

The cohort includes serial arms, a quadruped, a dexterous hand, a mobile
manipulator, and a bimanual manipulator. SO-101 and Unitree Go2 contribute six
reference-seen cells, while the remaining configurations contribute 27
transfer cells; these labels describe reference exposure rather than a transfer
effect. Source checks, behavioural validation, and downstream Task Demo answer
different questions and are reported separately. All resulting evidence is
limited to the declared MuJoCo models and conditions
\citep{todorov2012mujoco}.

The following sections organise the available morphology-specific evidence for
fixed serial arms, quadrupeds, an aerial quadrotor, and a humanoid biped. The
reported studies use different tasks, controllers, validation suites, and
attempt budgets. Their outcomes are stated at the relevant evidence level and
do not alter the 33-cell denominator defined above.

The onboarding evaluation combined structural checks with smoke-level
behavioural tests. A stricter re-evaluation retained the structural results but
exposed missing or insufficient behavioural checks on several drivers. The
sections below therefore state separately whether a result concerns source and
interface construction, robot-level behavioural validation, or downstream task
execution.

Code as Policies (CaP) generates executable policy programs that can process
perception outputs and compose supplied control APIs
\citep{liang2023codeaspolicies}. In the comparisons reported here, CaP
receives the same robot-facing capability interface as Auto-Adapter but
expresses the complete task as a single generated program. Auto-Adapter
instead uses that interface through a multi-turn controller. The comparison
therefore concerns downstream task control over a fixed interface, not the
preceding synthesis of that interface.


\section[Experiment Design]{Experiment Design}
\label{sec:cross-robot-experiment-design}

Experiment~3 comprises four configuration-specific evidence blocks: serial
arms, quadrupeds, the Skydio X2 quadrotor, and the Unitree H1 humanoid. The
blocks do not share one driver-generation model, task suite, validation
procedure, repetition count, or attempt budget. Each outcome therefore belongs
only to the protocol that produced it. Configurations without a downstream
evaluation, including KUKA iiwa 14 and Unitree A1, are recorded as not
evaluated at that level rather than counted as task failures.

The language model also has two distinct roles. In paired downstream
comparisons, Auto-Adapter supplies a fixed robot-specific driver and capability
interface, while ReCAP and CaP use that same software boundary. In the
quadruped comparison, the driver was generated with Sonnet 4.5, while the
downstream language-model backbone was either Sonnet 4.5 or Sonnet 4.6. The
retained Skydio X2 and H1 drivers were also generated with Sonnet 4.5, whereas
their separate cross-model sweeps regenerated the driver with two attempts per
model. The SO-101 cross-model study followed a different protocol and is
reported separately.

The chapter distinguishes structural and smoke-level onboarding, stricter
driver validation, downstream task trials, and cross-model synthesis attempts.
A downstream success does not change the preceding driver-validation verdict,
and task performance reported only for validating drivers remains conditional
on successful synthesis. Patched or selected drivers are identified in their
respective sections and are not presented as untouched first-attempt
generation.

Results are analysed within each evidence block using their local units and
denominators. Cross-block interpretation is qualitative and concerns the
location of observed successes and failures. The analysis does not pool the
results into a common robot-level rate or use them to infer a causal morphology
ordering.


\section[Serial-Arm Manipulation]{Serial-Arm Manipulation}
\label{sec:cross-robot-serial-arms}

The evaluated cohort contained five fixed serial arms: SO-101, Piper, UR5e,
Franka Panda, and KUKA iiwa 14. A self-contained driver was generated for each
configuration. The initial onboarding suite reported seven of seven checks
passing for every arm. A stricter re-evaluation retained the
source, build, and homing results, but the five drivers lacked a
scene-perception operation added to the revised harness. The original
onboarding result should therefore be interpreted as structural and
smoke-level evidence rather than as equivalent to candidate-independent
Capability Validation.

The depth of downstream evaluation differed substantially across the five
arms. SO-101 supported the broadest manipulation and model-portability
experiments. Piper and UR5e were evaluated on the same five-task mixed
physics-and-behaviour suite, Franka was examined through a controlled reach
breakdown, and KUKA received onboarding tests without a downstream task
evaluation. These unequal protocols preclude a pooled serial-arm success rate.

On Piper and UR5e, Auto-Adapter completed 12 of 15 trials per robot
($80\%$), compared with 9 of 15 ($60\%$) for CaP. Both methods completed the
basic reach and gripper tasks and failed the multi-waypoint square trace. The
difference arose from \texttt{unreachable\_detect}: Auto-Adapter reported the
inverse-kinematics exception correctly in all three trials, whereas CaP
reported completion in all three despite the failed command. A separate Piper
pick experiment completed 30 of 30 task trials.
Figure~\ref{fig:piper-pick-rollout} traces one retained rollout from the home
pose through approach and descent to grasp and a $0.12$\,m lift. The final
frame shows the green object raised above the table, distinguishing pickup from
contact alone. Because the driver included a 13-line post-synthesis patch, this
result supports feasibility of the resulting interface under the evaluated
scene, not untouched end-to-end generation.

Franka exposed a different limitation. Across seven Cartesian reach variants
with ten trials each, the driver passed 20 of 70 trials ($28.6\%$), with a
mean final error of $3.49$\,cm. The positive $x$ and positive $z$ variants
passed all trials, four directions ended approximately $2$--$4$\,cm from
their targets, and the negative $z$ variant ended $11.07$\,cm away at the
workspace and joint-limit boundary. A deterministic scripted oracle with no
language model reproduced the same two-of-seven variant pattern. Increasing
the damped-least-squares iteration budget fivefold and introducing null-space
regularisation did not improve the aggregate count, while
gravity-compensated execution recovered one additional variant. The observed
ceiling therefore lies in the prescribed control template and execution path,
rather than in the language model's selection of the Cartesian target alone.

The cross-model comparison used SO-101 grasping as the contact-rich
manipulation reference. Task success among self-written drivers that passed
validation ranged from $0.28$ to $0.46$.
Because its task, validation, and synthesis-attempt protocol differed from the
non-gripper experiments below, this range is retained as context rather than
as a directly comparable morphology-level estimate.


\section[Quadruped Locomotion]{Quadruped Locomotion}
\label{sec:cross-robot-quadrupeds}

The evaluated quadruped configurations were Unitree Go2, Unitree A1, and
ANYmal-C, each with twelve actuators. All three passed the structural and
smoke-level onboarding suite. Under stricter locomotion checks, however, Go2
did not lower its body sufficiently during
\texttt{sit}, A1 moved backwards during \texttt{walk\_forward}, and ANYmal-C
failed the revised standing or displacement requirements. A1 consequently
received no downstream task result. The Go2 and ANYmal-C results below came
from a separate ten-task suite with independent physics grading and do not
inherit the leniency of the onboarding checks.

walking increment was comparatively coarse. These observations describe
interaction between each driver, controller, and task suite; they do not
isolate a causal effect of quadruped morphology.

The two-attempt cross-model synthesis sweep produced a validated Go2 driver
in one of two attempts for Opus 4.8 and one of two attempts for Sonnet 4.6.
Their downstream task-success rates were $0.70$ and $0.82$, respectively.
The other five model configurations produced no validating driver within two
attempts. These counts measure reliability under the bounded two-attempt
protocol and do not support a model ranking.


\section[Aerial Quadrotor]{Aerial Quadrotor}
\label{sec:cross-robot-aerial}

Skydio X2 extended the eight-robot core benchmark to an aerial configuration.
Its floating base and four thrust rotors make the system underactuated and
open-loop unstable. The generated driver exposed \texttt{takeoff},
\texttt{move\_to}, \texttt{hover}, \texttt{land}, and
\texttt{get\_base\_pose}. Internally, it implemented a cascaded
position-to-attitude-to-rotor-thrust feedback controller rather than relying
on kinematics alone.

The Sonnet 4.5-generated driver passed all seven class-specific onboarding
checks, including commanded-altitude take-off while upright and movement to a
world-frame target without overturning. The corrected downstream comparison
used eight physics-graded tasks with five trials per task. The suite covered
take-off, altitude holding, point-to-point movement, return movement, diagonal
flight, and square patrol, and was tightened so that a do-nothing controller
passed none of its eight tasks. Auto-Adapter and same-interface CaP each
completed all 40 task trials. The result therefore supports successful
synthesis and validation of the flight controller under those conditions; it
does not support an advantage for the Auto-Adapter agent loop.

Figure~\ref{fig:skydio-flight-rollout} illustrates one multi-stage rollout
from this suite. Its five frames represent take-off to $0.5$\,m, translation
to $(0.4,0,0.5)$, return to the starting horizontal position, and landing.
The sequence clarifies the controlled stages, but as one example it adds no
comparative evidence beyond the aggregate 40-trial outcomes.

In the two-attempt cross-model sweep, Opus 4.8 and Sonnet 4.6 each produced
validating drivers in both attempts. Haiku 4.5 and DeepSeek V3.2 each
succeeded once, while Nova Pro, Ministral 8B, and Qwen3 32B produced no
validating driver. The downstream task-success rates of the passing drivers
were $1.00$ for Opus, $0.95$ for Sonnet, $1.00$ for Haiku, and $0.82$ for
DeepSeek. These results are configuration-specific outcomes because the
two-attempt sweep does not estimate a general aerial synthesis rate.


\section[Humanoid Biped]{Humanoid Biped}
\label{sec:cross-robot-humanoid}

Unitree H1 provided the humanoid case. The evaluated model had 19 actuated
joints and a floating torso, and it fell without active stabilisation. The
retained Sonnet 4.5 driver was the third synthesis version and implemented a
joint-space proportional--derivative balance controller.
It passed all seven class-specific validation checks. During the standing
test, torso height remained approximately $0.98$\,m and the body-axis upright
measure remained $1.00$. During squat recovery, the torso lowered and then
returned to approximately $0.97$\,m with an upright measure of $0.99$.

The result was repeated under the home-initialised protocol: standing passed
four of four runs and squat recovery passed three of three. This evidence is
reported at the driver-validation level because the orchestrator advanced
physics directly during the stand and squat checks. A replay that failed to
advance physics could allow a homed robot to remain upright without executing
the requested controller, so controller-reported completion alone would not
support the same claim.

Figure~\ref{fig:h1-squat-recovery} visualises the validated
squat-and-recovery motion. Across five frames, H1 lowers its torso while
retaining an upright posture and then returns close to its initial standing
height. The sequence illustrates simulator-level driver validation; it does
not show dynamic walking.

Dynamic walking remained a negative result. Attempts to generate a
closed-loop micro-step gait did not produce a stable,
validator-certified walk, and adding the walking objective disrupted the
previously reliable standing behaviour. The evidence therefore supports
static balance and squat recovery under the evaluated simulation conditions,
but not dynamic bipedal locomotion.

The two-attempt cross-model sweep should not be conflated with the retained
Sonnet 4.5 driver above. In that sweep, only Opus 4.8 produced a
validating H1 driver, in one of two attempts, and its downstream task-success
rate was $0.50$. The remaining model configurations produced no validating
driver. This was the lowest validation frequency among the three
non-gripper configurations in that sweep, but the tasks, controllers, and
dynamics changed together. It therefore remains a descriptive result rather
than evidence of a causal morphology ordering.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

This chapter examined the available MuJoCo evidence for serial arms,
quadrupeds, Skydio X2, and Unitree H1. The studies showed both feasibility and
failure at distinct evidence levels. For serial arms, source, build, and homing
checks were retained, but stricter evaluation exposed missing perception and a
control-and-execution ceiling on Franka. Quadruped performance depended on the
evaluated robot and controller; neither Auto-Adapter nor CaP dominated both
configurations. On Skydio X2, both controllers completed all 40 task trials,
supporting the flight interface without establishing an agent-loop advantage.
Unitree H1 supported standing and squat recovery at the driver-validation
level, but not validated dynamic walking.

The studies used different tasks, validation suites, controllers, and attempt
budgets. Their results therefore cannot be pooled into a common success rate or
interpreted as a causal morphology ordering. Taken together, the evidence
supports configuration-specific execution within the evaluated MuJoCo
settings; it does not establish general robot integration, hardware behaviour,
or sim-to-real transfer.
