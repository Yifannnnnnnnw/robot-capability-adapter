\chapter[Cross-Robot Evaluation]{Cross-Robot Evaluation}
\label{chapter:cross-robot-evaluation}

\begin{quote}\itshape
This chapter examines Auto-Adapter across robots with different morphologies.
It introduces the research questions and relevant background, then presents the
experimental design and results. The analysis considers driver validation and
performance on specific tasks. It also examines differences between robot
configurations and discusses the limitations of the comparison.
\end{quote}

\section[Introduction]{Introduction}
\label{sec:cross-robot-introduction}

The practical value of automated driver synthesis depends on its applicability
across different robots. A driver that works for one robot may need substantial
changes to support another. Cross-robot evaluation therefore examines whether
the same generation process can satisfy different implementation requirements.

Chapter 3 introduced the complete Auto-Adapter framework. Chapter 4 separately
evaluated driver synthesis and downstream driver use. This chapter asks whether
Auto-Adapter can generate usable drivers for robots with different morphologies.
It also examines how the generated drivers perform on specific tasks.

The analysis assesses driver behaviour and downstream task completion
separately. It follows the process from capability design through driver
generation and behavioural validation. Execution records help identify where
failures occur and how they affect task performance. Revision behaviour and
resource use provide additional context for interpreting differences between
robots.

\section[Background]{Background}
\label{sec:cross-robot-background}

Robot structure affects how an operation is translated into motion. Fixed-base
arms can have different reachable workspaces and require different joint
configurations to reach a target. A similar positioning request may therefore
require different implementations across robots \citep{lynch2017modern}.

Contact and stability introduce further requirements. For a multi-finger hand,
reaching specified finger positions does not establish that an object has been
grasped. Grasping also depends on the contacts between the fingers and the
object \citep{lynch2017modern}. Behavioural requirements must therefore
reflect the robot and the operation being evaluated.

Drivers connect to the robot's existing control software and translate
capability calls into executable control commands
\citep{pantano2022capabilitysurvey}. Auto-Adapter therefore derives capabilities
and measurable behavioural requirements from robot information and target
tasks. For comparison, all robots use the same generation model and resource
budget. Behavioural tests assess the operations required by each robot.
Differences in task performance are interpreted alongside the task requirements
and the robot's control method.

\section{Experimental Configuration}

The experiments used Claude Opus 5 for Auto-Adapter and GPT-6 Astra for ReCAP
in MuJoCo. Three robot case studies are reported: Franka Panda, LEAP Hand and
Skydio X2, representing a fixed-base arm, a dexterous hand and an aerial robot,
respectively. Each case assessed one generated driver across capability tests
and multiple downstream tasks.

This experiment evaluated capability
design and driver validation across the selected robots. ReCAP used
the generated drivers to perform tasks selected from each robot's task
library to cover its principal capabilities
(Table~\ref{tab:appendix-task-libraries}).
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-evaluation-criteria} summarises selected measurement
and timing requirements. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cross-robot-review}
records reassessments, retries and additional preparation for downstream execution.

Although the generation model and nominal resource budget were held constant,
downstream evaluation required robot-specific post-generation preparation in
some cases, as documented in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cross-robot-review}.
The cross-robot comparison should therefore be interpreted as a set of case
studies rather than a controlled morphology comparison.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:cross-robot-results-discussion}

\subsection{Franka Panda}
\label{sec:cross-robot-panda}

In this initial case study, Auto-Adapter generated a Panda driver using
Claude Opus 5. Its \texttt{end-effector positioning}, \texttt{ordered waypoints},
\texttt{gripper aperture} and \texttt{wrist rotation} capabilities met their
respective test criteria. The recorded positioning and waypoint errors were
5.30 and 6.04\,mm, respectively, both below the revised common acceptance
threshold of 20\,mm. ReCAP used GPT-6 Astra to combine these operations
for reaching, pushing, pick-and-place, drawer opening and dial turning. All
five tasks passed in each of three spatial layouts, giving 15/15 successful
executions, demonstrating reuse across their motion and contact requirements
(Figure~\ref{fig:cross-robot-panda-tasks}).

The \texttt{contact pressing} test exposed a mismatch between the capability
description and validation criteria. The end effector reached the required
position, but contact occurred at the hand while force measurement targeted
the left fingertip pad. The specified contact-pair force criterion
was therefore not met. The peak-force check also failed to establish sustained
contact throughout the holding phase or enforce the requested force limit.
Assessing capability generation thus requires examining both the implementation
and the test definition: positional accuracy alone does not establish the
specified force-control behaviour.

\subsection{LEAP Hand}
\label{sec:cross-robot-leap}

Auto-Adapter generated five capabilities for LEAP Hand:
\texttt{joint pose}, \texttt{fingertip positioning},
\texttt{ordered waypoints}, \texttt{contact force} and
\texttt{synchronised flexion hold}. Four of six validation cases passed.
Positioning and path errors were 2.44 and 0.30\,mm, below their respective
12 and 15\,mm limits. These results indicate precise motion of the tested
finger link.

Using GPT-6 Astra, ReCAP attempted fingertip reaching, joint pose matching,
object holding, block manipulation and valve turning. The first evaluation
returned 0/5 successes. A separate retry using the same driver passed
fingertip reaching, with an error of 8.08\,mm at the required 2\,s endpoint,
below the 8.94\,mm limit. The other four tasks remained unsuccessful.

Figure~\ref{fig:cross-robot-leap-tasks} illustrates the distinction between
local motion and object manipulation. The first pose-matching attempt
ended with an error of only $1.37^\circ$, but stopped at 3.924\,s before
the required 4\,s evaluation endpoint. Object holding did not keep the
object in place. At the evaluation endpoint, the block's position error
was 35.73\,mm against a 10\,mm limit, while the valve's angular error was
3.12\,rad against a 0.10\,rad limit. Small local motion errors therefore
did not establish complete multi-finger task execution.

Validation coverage helps interpret this gap. Positioning and path tests
measured only the index link, while joint pose validation covered two of
the 16 joints, leaving whole-hand coordination insufficiently assessed.
The contact force test used a peak criterion and did not establish sustained
contact. Synchronised flexion required 0.30\,rad movement while allowing
only 0.05\,rad deviation from the initial pose, creating a conflict between
the requested action and its criterion. This case exposes limitations in
object manipulation and shows that validation needs to cover sustained
contact, whole-hand coordination and the complete execution period.

\subsection{Skydio X2}
\label{sec:cross-robot-skydio}

Auto-Adapter generated five capabilities for Skydio X2:
\texttt{station holding}, \texttt{point positioning},
\texttt{ordered waypoints}, \texttt{heading alignment} and
\texttt{controlled descent}. Four of five capability tests passed.
Positioning and path errors were 23.93 and 10.38\,mm, both below the
25\,mm limit.\footnote{The distance thresholds and the 5/5 task result
in this section use a retrospective assessment under the revised criteria
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cross-robot-review}).}
The heading alignment test recorded a reference-point position error of
349.84\,mm, exceeding the 190\,mm limit.

Using GPT-6 Astra, ReCAP executed takeoff and hover, stationary holding,
climb--transit--descend return, ordered waypoints and point-of-interest
orbiting. All five tasks passed, giving 5/5 successes.
Figure~\ref{fig:cross-robot-skydio-tasks} shows execution frames from four
of these tasks.

  ordered waypoints and the first point-of-interest orbit attempt.

The orbit task further examined coordination between position and heading
control. Figure~\ref{fig:cross-robot-skydio-orbit}(a) shows that the first
trajectory approximated the required 1.5\,m radius circle and completed a
full circuit. Maximum radius and height errors were 60.21 and 12.04\,mm,
below their respective 100 and 50\,mm limits. The root-mean-square error
in heading towards the circle centre was $10.54^\circ$, within the
$12^\circ$ limit.

The separate retry also completed a full circuit, but its root-mean-square
heading error was $13.75^\circ$, exceeding the $12^\circ$ limit.
Both trajectories approximated the target circle, yet their heading errors
differed. The generated capabilities supported the orbit path, while
maintaining the required orientation towards the centre remained
inconsistent. This case therefore requires assessment of both trajectory
and heading accuracy; path geometry alone is insufficient to evaluate
the complete flight-control behaviour.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:cross-robot-discussion}

The three cases show that positioning and path capabilities generated by
Auto-Adapter can provide basic operations for tasks on different robots.
ReCAP combined these operations across multiple Panda and Skydio tasks,
whereas LEAP's local positioning accuracy did not translate into reliable
object manipulation. This difference indicates that a capability's task
value depends on whether it can work with other operations to meet
requirements for sustained contact, coordinated motion and orientation
control. The accuracy of an individual motion therefore needs to be
interpreted alongside performance on the complete task.

Validation definitions and test coverage established during Design also
affect capability evaluation. Mismatches between the measured and actual
contact locations, the use of peak force in place of sustained force, and
tests covering only a few joints all limit the interpretation of validation
results. These issues indicate that capability evaluation needs to assess
consistency between the driver implementation, capability requirements and
test definitions. These cases reinforce the need for human review of
validation methods and thresholds established during Design. This review
checks whether the tests accurately express the required behaviour.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:cross-robot-summary}

This chapter evaluated Franka Panda, LEAP Hand and Skydio X2 in MuJoCo,
examining Auto-Adapter's capability design, driver validation and task
execution through ReCAP. The generated Panda and Skydio drivers supported
multiple manipulation and flight tasks, while LEAP achieved accurate local
motion but remained limited in object manipulation. These results support
the use of Auto-Adapter to generate reusable capabilities that support tasks
across the tested robot morphologies, while revealing limitations in
sustained contact, coordinated control and validation design.
