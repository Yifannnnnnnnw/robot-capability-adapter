\chapter[Background and Literature Review]{Background and Literature Review}
\label{chapter:background}

\begin{quote}\itshape
The purpose of this chapter is to establish the shared conceptual and
methodological foundations for the empirical studies in this thesis. The first
part positions the robot software synthesised in this thesis—reusable robot
capability layers and their robot-specific drivers—within embodied AI and
robotic systems. The second part reviews LLM coding agents and research on
using LLMs to generate, validate, and revise robot-control software. The
chapter concludes by deriving the research gaps examined in Chapters~3--5.
\end{quote}

The literature relevant to Auto-Adapter is distributed across robotics,
machine learning, software engineering, programming languages, and language
agents. A review organised only around recent model families would obscure the
research object, because Auto-Adapter does not propose a new policy, VLA, or
task planner. Conversely, a review restricted to device drivers would miss the
reason a reusable robot interface has become increasingly important: a growing
range of downstream systems expects such an interface to exist.

The chapter therefore follows the lifecycle of a robot capability adapter.
It first asks what layer is being generated and which information that layer
must preserve. It then considers who consumes the layer, how comparable
software artefacts have been synthesised, how generated robot code has been
tested and repaired, and why validation itself must not become a new
per-robot engineering bottleneck. This organisation keeps policy generation,
primitive implementation, runtime binding, and behavioural judgement as
separate research problems.


\section[Application Domain]{Application Domain: Robot Capability Interfaces}
\label{sec:application-domain}

\subsection[Robot Execution Stack]{The Path from Robot Intelligence to Execution}
\label{sec:intelligence-to-execution}

A robot task begins with an objective and ends with a physical state change,
but several software layers intervene between these points. An objective may
be encoded as a natural-language instruction, reward, goal image, symbolic
state, or reference trajectory. A policy or planner converts that objective
and current observations into an action. The action is interpreted by a
robot-specific execution interface, passed through middleware or an SDK, and
ultimately realised by a controller and physical mechanism.

The output of the intelligence layer varies. A learned policy may produce
joint targets, end-effector displacements, velocities, or action chunks. A VLA
model may decode continuous actions from language and visual observations. An
agentic controller may select a named skill or generate a program that invokes
robot functions. These outputs differ in abstraction and frequency, but none
is self-executing. A number such as $0.1$ can represent metres, radians,
normalised motor range, velocity, or gripper closure depending on the
interface. A call such as \texttt{home()} has no physical semantics until an
implementation defines target state, motion order, tolerance, timeout,
readback, and failure behaviour.

The thesis uses the following conceptual stack:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.35em]
  \item a task objective and environment observation;
  \item a policy, VLA, planner, or task agent;
  \item a stable consumer-facing action or primitive contract;
  \item an embodiment- and runtime-specific capability adapter;
  \item middleware, vendor SDK, transport, and controller; and
  \item the simulator or physical robot.
\end{enumerate}

Auto-Adapter operates primarily at Layers 3 and 4. It takes a declared
capability surface and produces the robot-specific artefact that gives that
surface an executable meaning. The surrounding layers are important because
they provide inputs, consumers, and evidence, but they are not collapsed into
the generated adapter.

\subsection[Terminology]{Actions, Primitives, Skills, Controllers, and Adapters}
\label{sec:terminology}

Robotics uses overlapping terms for behaviour at different temporal and
software scales. A precise vocabulary is needed because an apparent novelty at
one level may be established practice at another.

The word \emph{primitive} has at least two relevant traditions. In motor
control, a movement primitive can denote a parameterised motion generator;
dynamical movement primitives, for example, represent motor behaviours as
stable dynamical systems with learned forcing terms
\citep{ijspeert2013dmp}. In capability-oriented robot software, a primitive is
instead an atomic executable unit beneath a skill or task. A structured review
of 210 industrial robot-skill papers finds broad convergence on a
task--skill--primitive hierarchy, while also documenting substantial variation
in terminology and representation \citep{pantano2022capabilitysurvey}.
Auto-Adapter uses the second meaning. Its primitive may invoke a trajectory
generator or controller, but it is defined by the software contract exposed to
a caller rather than by one particular motion representation.

A \emph{policy} maps an observation, and optionally a goal or instruction, to
an action. It may be learned from reward, demonstrations, or heterogeneous
pre-training data. A \emph{task program} represents decision logic explicitly,
for example as code, a behaviour tree, or a sequence of tool calls. A
\emph{skill} is a temporally extended behaviour such as grasping, navigating
to a location, or opening a drawer. Skills may be learned, scripted, or
composed from lower-level operations. Skill frameworks already use explicit
parameters and pre-, hold-, and postconditions to support planning and reactive
execution; SkiROS2 is a representative example \citep{mayr2023skiros2}. Thus,
neither the hierarchy nor behavioural conditions are claimed as novel here.
The research question concerns the embodiment- and runtime-specific
implementation and qualification beneath such abstractions.

A \emph{controller} owns a feedback loop that converts a reference into
actuator commands. Its update frequency, latency, stability, and safety
requirements often make it unsuitable for direct implementation by a
general-purpose language model. An approved vendor or simulator controller can
therefore remain beneath the layer generated in this thesis.

A \emph{primitive} is the smallest stable operation exposed by the capability
layer to a downstream caller. Stability here refers to semantics rather than
duration: a bounded joint command may be short and non-blocking, while a homing
primitive may own a longer completion loop. A primitive contract specifies:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.25em]
  \item its name, arguments, return value, and errors;
  \item physical units and coordinate frames;
  \item valid inputs, limits, and safety preconditions;
  \item whether the caller or primitive owns the feedback loop;
  \item observable postconditions and required evidence; and
  \item lifecycle, timeout, cleanup, and failure semantics.
\end{itemize}

A \emph{primitive implementation} realises one contract operation for a
particular robot and route. A \emph{capability adapter} is the reusable
software artefact that implements a suite of such primitives. A simulator
driver that implements its own inverse kinematics or gait is one form of
capability adapter. A thin runtime adapter that binds the same contract to an
existing vendor controller is another. The unifying property is not code
thickness, but the stable interface presented to downstream consumers.

The adapter excludes task-specific compositions such as
\texttt{pick\_cube()}, \texttt{place\_in\_drawer()}, or
\texttt{inspect\_shelf()}. Encoding benchmark tasks inside the adapter would
make synthesis appear successful by moving task logic into the supposedly
reusable layer. It also excludes certified braking and low-level torque loops,
which remain the responsibility of approved runtime components. These
boundaries allow adapter reuse to be evaluated independently of both task
planning and firmware control.


\subsection[Robot Software and Runtime Routes]{Robot Software Abstractions and Runtime Routes}
\label{sec:robot-software-stack}

Robot software frameworks have long sought to isolate reusable algorithms from
device-specific implementation. ROS introduced a modular, message-oriented
ecosystem for composing perception, planning, and control components
\citep{quigley2009ros}. ROS~2 extended this architecture with managed
lifecycle, multiple communication qualities of service, and stronger support
for production deployments \citep{macenski2022ros2}. Planning frameworks and
controller managers further define common interfaces above hardware-specific
plugins.

The \texttt{ros2\_control} architecture illustrates both the benefit and limit
of standardisation. Reusable controllers interact with declared state and
command interfaces, but a new device still requires a hardware component that
implements initialisation, lifecycle transitions, state reads, and command
writes \citep{ros2control2026hardware}. LeRobot similarly defines a common
robot interface for dataset collection and policy execution, while its
bring-your-own-hardware procedure asks the integrator to implement connection,
calibration, observation features, action features, command transmission, and
safety constraints \citep{lerobot2026hardware}. The abstraction is shared;
its executable realisation is robot-specific.

An adapter may reach the robot through several \emph{runtime routes}. A
simulator route calls a physics-engine API and may expose complete state. A
software or transport shim exercises the same SDK-facing calls while a
simulator or bridge supplies observations. A middleware route may publish ROS
messages or invoke actions and services. A hardware route may call a vendor
SDK, communicate through CAN or serial transport, or contact a controller
service. These routes differ not only in latency and fidelity but also in what
can be observed and therefore what can be validated.

Protocol bridges occupy a related but distinct layer. They describe how an
agent discovers or invokes capabilities that already exist in a robot stack.
RoboNeuron derives agent-facing tools from ROS schemas, and the Robot Context
Protocol proposes a runtime-agnostic interface for agent-aware control
\citep{roboneuron2025,rcp2025}. Such bridges improve access to an existing
capability, but a message schema alone does not establish whether its units,
joint mapping, calibration, or physical postcondition are correct. Interface
exposure and interface implementation should therefore not be conflated.


\subsection[Specification Sources]{Structural Description, Runtime Profile, and Primitive Contract}
\label{sec:specification-sources}

Auto-Adapter separates three sources of specification that are often embedded
implicitly in hand-written integration code.

First, a robot description such as URDF or MJCF represents morphology. It can
record links, joints, limits, inertial properties, collision geometry,
actuators, and sensors. MJCF also provides an executable model for the MuJoCo
physics engine \citep{todorov2012mujoco}. These descriptions allow software to
infer kinematic structure and constraints without relying entirely on prose.

Second, a runtime profile represents the deployment route. It identifies the
approved SDK or middleware surface, installed packages, joint and device
bindings, command and observation schemas, absolute or relative semantics,
calibration resources, control rate, available sensing, lifecycle, and safety
limits. These properties cannot generally be inferred from geometry. Two
deployments of the same robot may use different profiles, while two robots
with similar kinematics may have incompatible runtime semantics.

Third, the primitive contract specifies what downstream software expects. It
defines stable behaviour independently of the structural and runtime details
used to implement that behaviour. The same arm contract might be instantiated
through simulator actuators, a ROS action, or a vendor SDK, provided that each
route supplies compatible semantics and evidence.

The separation establishes a stable--variable boundary. Primitive semantics
are human-designed and reused. Morphology and runtime facts vary by robot and
route. The generated adapter binds the two. This is more constrained than
asking an LLM to invent an arbitrary robot program, but less labour-intensive
than hand-writing every binding if the specifications contain enough
information.


\subsection[Execution Evidence]{Correctness, Observability, and Execution Evidence}
\label{sec:execution-evidence}

Robot-facing software can be correct at one layer and incorrect at another. A
file may be syntactically valid but export the wrong method. A method may
satisfy its Python signature while interpreting degrees as radians. A command
may cross the intended transport while addressing the wrong joint. The correct
command may reach a controller without producing motion because of calibration
error, deadband, stiction, an inactive servo, collision, or a stale observation
route. A useful evidence model must preserve these distinctions.

This thesis uses four broad levels of qualification:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=nextline,itemsep=0.55em]
  \item[\textbf{Static and source evidence.}]
  Syntax, imports, required artefacts, allowed dependencies, and prohibited
  operations are checked before execution.

  \item[\textbf{Interface and contract evidence.}]
  Signatures, aliases, units, frames, bounds, lifecycle, error behaviour, and
  declared observations are checked against the primitive contract.

  \item[\textbf{Transport evidence.}]
  Commands and observations are shown to pass through the declared route with
  the expected schema and without bypassing safety checks.

  \item[\textbf{Behavioural evidence.}]
  Observed state is compared with the postcondition of the commanded
  primitive, for example through joint displacement, end-effector error,
  gripper aperture, contact, image availability, or recovery state.
\end{description}

Behavioural evidence is bounded by observability. Simulator state may provide
joint, pose, and contact facts that are unavailable on hardware. Hardware
readback may reveal stiction and transport delay absent from simulation, while
lacking a reliable task-success label. An obligation that cannot be observed
should be reported as blocked or unassessed; absence of evidence should not be
converted into a pass.

The party that records facts should also be distinguished from the party that
judges them. An environment executor can record command, observation, time,
route, and artefact identity. A validator applies thresholds and contract
semantics to those facts. Keeping verdict language out of raw evidence enables
replay, alternative analysis, and clearer responsibility, while preventing the
generating model from becoming its own judge.


\section[Literature Review]{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature-review}

\subsection[Robot Capabilities]{Robot Capabilities and Skill Abstractions}
\label{sec:lit-capabilities}

Capability-based robot software predates LLM-generated code. Its central aim
is to separate reusable descriptions of what a robot can do from the concrete
programs that realise those descriptions. Pantano et al.'s structured review
finds that most industrial skill frameworks can be organised around tasks,
skills, and primitives, while differing in how parameters, world state, and
hardware dependencies are represented \citep{pantano2022capabilitysurvey}.
This literature provides the conceptual ancestry of the primitive contract
used in this thesis; Auto-Adapter does not claim the hierarchy itself as a new
ontology.

SkiROS2 shows how explicit parameters and pre-, hold-, and postconditions can
support task planning, reactive execution, and skill composition
\citep{mayr2023skiros2}. Such conditions are useful both for selecting a skill
and for checking its execution. The platform nevertheless assumes that the
skills, resource managers, and low-level ROS capabilities needed by a concrete
robot have been implemented. It standardises the semantics above this
boundary, but does not automatically instantiate the boundary for each concrete
integration.

Capability-driven skill generation moves closer to automation. Silva et al.
encode capabilities in machine-readable form and use retrieval-augmented
generation over existing libraries and resource interfaces to produce ROS~2
skills \citep{silva2025capabilityskill}. This is an important precedent for
using a contract to guide robot code generation. Its generated skill still
builds on supplied ROS resources and libraries. The lower question retained by
Auto-Adapter is how the reusable joint, gripper, observation, lifecycle, and
transport primitives themselves are instantiated for a declared embodiment
and runtime, and how that implementation is qualified.

\subsection[Generalist Robot Policies]{Generalist Policies as Consumers of an Execution Interface}
\label{sec:lit-policies}

Learned robot policies address the problem of producing actions from
observations and goals. Diffusion Policy models multi-modal visuomotor action
distributions through diffusion, while Action Chunking with Transformers
predicts temporally extended action sequences for fine-grained bimanual
manipulation \citep{chi2023diffusionpolicy,zhao2023act}. These methods can be
highly effective within the action and observation space supplied by the
training and deployment environment. They do not aim to generate that
environment's hardware binding.

Robot foundation models broaden the scope of policy reuse. RT-1 and RT-2
demonstrate language-conditioned control at increasing data and model scales
\citep{brohan2022rt1,brohan2023rt2}. Open X-Embodiment aggregates trajectories
across many robot platforms and trains RT-X models on a coarsely aligned
seven-dimensional gripper-frame action representation
\citep{openxembodiment2024}. Octo, OpenVLA, and $\pi_0$ further investigate
open or generalist policies trained from heterogeneous robot data
\citep{octo2024,kim2024openvla,black2024pi0}.

These works make action heterogeneity a central modelling problem, but do not
eliminate the execution interface. Open X-Embodiment normalises actions per
dataset while retaining differences in coordinate frames and whether values
denote positions, deltas, or velocities. Octo supports a new action space by
adapting its output head and fine-tuning on a target setup. UniVLA learns
task-centric latent actions across embodiments, but executable control still
requires a robot-specific decoder \citep{bu2025univla}. Recent deployment
analysis shows why this distinction matters: the same VLA checkpoint can
produce physically different policies when unnormalisation metadata or
controller conventions change, as illustrated in a recent deployment-focused
preprint \citep{tai2026sameweights}.

These methods address generalisation above an already defined action boundary
rather than constructing that boundary. A policy can transfer
perception and action knowledge only after its output has been assigned valid
semantics on the target robot. Auto-Adapter treats policies and VLAs as
potential consumers of a capability adapter and focuses on the executable
binding that their evaluations normally supply in advance.


\subsection[Agentic Robotics]{Agentic Robotics and Code over Existing Capabilities}
\label{sec:lit-agentic}

Language-model robotics often operates at a higher semantic level. SayCan
combines an LLM's task knowledge with affordance values associated with
pre-trained robot skills \citep{ahn2022saycan}. This grounds planning in what
the particular robot can execute, but the skill policies and their value
functions are inputs to the method. The contribution is skill selection and
composition, not construction of the underlying execution layer.

Code as Policies generates executable programs that manipulate perception
outputs, perform geometric reasoning, and call existing robot control APIs
\citep{liang2023codeaspolicies}. The program can express richer logic than a
flat skill sequence, yet it still depends on a supplied interface. RoboCodeX
similarly generates task behaviour using multimodal reasoning and existing
planning and control utilities \citep{mu2024robocodex}. These works show that
code is an effective representation for task policy, while also exposing the
importance of the primitives made available to the code generator.

VoxPoser explicitly identifies predefined motion primitives as a bottleneck
and moves generation towards control by composing three-dimensional value maps
and producing end-effector trajectories \citep{huang2023voxposer}. It avoids
requiring a named macro for every task, but its trajectories still rely on a
known robot model, motion optimisation, inverse kinematics, and an operational
space controller. The result narrows the gap between task intent and motion,
without generating the controller and runtime binding for a declared robot--runtime integration.

Recent preprints extend code-based robotics with iterative improvement. CaP-X
reports a benchmark of coding agents under different robot API abstractions
and finds that human-designed scaffolding strongly affects task performance
\citep{fu2026capx}. RHO searches over multi-file robot policy repositories
using environment feedback while retaining supplied low-level primitives
\citep{elmaaroufi2026rho}. ASPIRE diagnoses failures from multimodal traces,
repairs task programs, and accumulates successful behaviours in a reusable
skill library \citep{lu2026aspire}. These systems demonstrate
increasingly capable automation above the primitive API. They also make the
boundary studied by Auto-Adapter sharper: reusable task skills can grow only
within the capabilities and runtime semantics exposed beneath them.


\subsection[Interface Generation]{Robot Middleware, Skills, and Interface Generation}
\label{sec:lit-interface-generation}

Middleware and skill frameworks seek reuse through stable contracts. ROS and
ROS~2 provide communication and lifecycle abstractions, while controller and
planning frameworks provide standard command surfaces
\citep{quigley2009ros,macenski2022ros2}. As discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:robot-software-stack}, their new-hardware procedures still
require a device-specific component. This is not an incidental omission: only
the integrator knows the concrete SDK, calibration, controller ownership, and
safety context unless those facts are made machine-readable.

ROS-LLM connects a language agent to ROS, supports several execution
representations, and can add atomic actions through imitation and feedback
\citep{mower2026rosllm}. It broadens who can program an existing robot and how
new skills enter a library, but continues to rely on an operational ROS stack,
controllers, and action implementations. RoboNeuron and RCP likewise improve
tool discovery and invocation above an existing runtime
\citep{roboneuron2025,rcp2025}. They are complementary consumers or exposure
layers for a generated adapter.

The literature therefore contains several partial forms of interface
automation: learning an action decoder, wrapping a message schema, learning a
demonstrated skill, or generating code over existing resources. The remaining
question is whether a declared suite of consumer-facing primitive
implementations can be synthesised and behaviourally qualified for a
declared robot--runtime integration from structural and runtime specifications.


\subsection[Program and Driver Synthesis]{Program, Tool, and Driver Synthesis}
\label{sec:lit-synthesis}

The synthesis problem has roots beyond recent LLMs. Program sketching combines
a partial program with a specification and searches for an implementation that
satisfies the specification
\citep{solarlezama2006combinatorial,solarlezama2013sketching}.
Counterexample-guided inductive synthesis follows a related
generate--check--refine pattern: a candidate is proposed, checked against a
specification, and revised using a counterexample. Auto-Adapter resembles this
shape, but its validator executes a finite set of execution tests rather than
proving a universally
quantified formal specification. The method should therefore be described as
specification-guided or CEGIS-inspired, not as formal synthesis with a
correctness proof.

Automatic driver generation also predates LLM coding agents. Termite-2
synthesises operating-system device drivers from device, operating-system, and
driver specifications, using abstraction refinement to diagnose incomplete
models \citep{ryzhyk2014termite}. It demonstrates that repetitive interface
implementation can be made a synthesis target and that specification quality
is part of the problem. Robot capability adapters differ in their continuous
state, kinematics, calibration, partial observability, contact, and physical
postconditions, but Termite prevents an overbroad claim that Auto-Adapter is
the first system to generate any kind of driver.

LLM tool-creation work replaces or supplements formal search with executable
code generation. Large Language Models as Tool Makers has a model produce a
reusable software function that another model can call, and CREATOR generates
tools to separate abstract reasoning from concrete computation
\citep{cai2023latm,qian2023creator}. Coding-agent work such as CodeAct and
SWE-agent shows how tool access, repository interaction, execution, and tests
support iterative code production \citep{wang2024codeact,yang2024sweagent}.
These methods justify treating the generated artefact as reusable and allowing
the agent to inspect its environment. Their common software benchmarks,
however, do not require commands to produce safe, observable physical state
transitions.

The initial Auto-Adapter study combines these ideas in the simulator domain:
an LLM agent reads MJCF or URDF, produces a reusable driver, and is checked
against simulator physics before a downstream agent uses the API
\citep{wang2026autoadapter}. The thesis retains this contribution as prior
evidence and develops the unresolved specification and assurance questions:
how runtime facts beyond morphology are represented, how a shared contract is
instantiated through an approved route, and how validation can scale without a
new hand-written oracle for each adapter.


\subsection[Test Oracles and Assurance]{Test Oracles and Robot Software Assurance}
\label{sec:lit-assurance}

Robot testing and assurance span model checking, simulation, scenario
generation, runtime monitoring, and testing at multiple system levels. A
systematic review of 192 robotic and autonomous-system studies finds diverse
models and interventions but limited shared adequacy measures and industrial
evidence \citep{araujo2023rasreview}. The present review therefore selects the
parts most directly relevant to generated capability adapters: property-based
input generation, runtime observation, simulation-based behavioural testing,
runtime assurance, and construction of the test oracle.

Testing generated code requires inputs, execution, observations, and an oracle
that decides whether the observations are acceptable. The oracle problem is
well established in software engineering: for many systems, correct output is
not available automatically for every input \citep{barr2015oracle}. In
robotics, the problem is intensified by continuous state, noise, partial
observation, asynchronous transport, and environmental variation. A function
returning without exception is not a sufficient behavioural oracle.

Property-based testing addresses part of the problem by expressing general
properties and generating test cases that seek counterexamples. QuickCheck
established this pattern for software functions \citep{claessen2000quickcheck}.
Property-based testing has subsequently been applied to ROS systems and to
robot actions in simulation \citep{santos2018pbtros,sohail2021pbt}. The latter
work generates scenes and checks action-execution properties, showing how
semantic robot failures can be revealed beyond a fixed demonstration.

Runtime verification provides another perspective. ROSRV generates monitors
for ROS message traffic from formal properties \citep{huang2014rosrv}. HAROS
extracts models from ROS source and supports static analysis, model checking,
runtime verification, and property-based testing \citep{santos2021haros}.
SOTER places uncertified high-performance components behind a runtime-assurance
architecture containing a certified safe controller and explicit safety
specification \citep{desai2019soter}. These systems demonstrate stronger
assurance models than ordinary testing. Auto-Adapter does not provide their
formal guarantees: its generated candidates are subject to bounded validation,
and vendor safety mechanisms remain beneath the adapter.

The relevant lesson is the separation of authority. The component under test
should not define its own success criterion, and a monitor should consume
facts rather than a candidate's claim of success. Auto-Adapter applies this
principle by separating environment execution and raw session facts from
deterministic verdicts. Here \emph{candidate-independent} means that the
generated adapter cannot alter the validator, thresholds, evidence record, or
final verdict, and that a repaired candidate is re-evaluated by the same
external gates. It does not mean that the contract and validator compiler were
designed by a different research organisation. At the same time, the thesis
confronts an additional scaling question: whether behavioural obligations can
be compiled from primitive contract $\times$ runtime profile $\times$ route
capabilities, so that external judgement does not require a second
robot-specific codebase as large as the adapter itself. The relevant measures
include robot-specific validator code, manual overrides, unobservable
obligations, and onboarding effort.


\subsection[Execution Feedback and Repair]{Execution Feedback and Program Repair}
\label{sec:lit-repair}

Iterative repair is common in both language agents and program synthesis.
Reflexion stores verbal feedback from previous attempts, CodeAct executes code
as an action, and SWE-agent interacts with repositories and tests
\citep{shinn2023reflexion,wang2024codeact,yang2024sweagent}. These approaches
show that an LLM can use external signals to revise an artefact, but also that
the content and independence of the signal matter.

Physics-based simulation is particularly useful in robotics because it offers
a safe, repeatable, and highly observable setting for development and testing
\citep{liu2021simulators}. Simulator state can expose joint motion, pose,
collision, and contact evidence that would be costly or unavailable on
hardware. Its value is nevertheless bounded by model, actuator, contact, and
sensor fidelity. Simulator measurements are therefore physics-grounded
execution evidence, not hardware evidence or a guarantee of sim-to-real
transfer.

Robot code repair introduces physical feedback. Tripathi et al. generate and
repair a navigation controller using structured tests
\citep{tripathi2026testdriven}. RoboInspector analyses failures of generated
manipulation policy code and uses execution feedback for refinement
\citep{ying2025roboinspector}. Neuro-symbolic Code-as-Policies combines
symbolic verification with interactive validation
\citep{ahn2025reliablecap}. ASPIRE records multimodal per-primitive traces,
uses them to diagnose and repair task programs, and retains successful
behaviours as skills \citep{lu2026aspire}. These methods assume an execution
engine that already exposes the underlying robot operations.

Kim et al. are a particularly close precedent for physics-guided correction.
Their closed-loop method detects collision, misalignment, pose, and contact
failures in simulation and iteratively corrects LLM-generated tool-use
primitives before real-robot execution \citep{kim2026physicalcorrection}.
Their target is a task-specific tool-use program and its motion parameters over
a fixed manipulation stack. Auto-Adapter instead targets a declared,
cross-task capability adapter for a declared embodiment and runtime route, and separately
asks who defines the acceptance oracle and how much per-robot validation work
remains. Physics feedback, iterative correction, and real-robot replay are
therefore not claimed as firsts. The proposed contribution lies in applying
candidate-independent, contract-derived evidence to the lower adapter layer
and measuring qualification reuse. A generic generate--test--repair loop is
consequently not novel by itself.

Auto-Adapter's repair question is specific to capability onboarding. A failed
primitive may involve the wrong joint order, sign, unit, frame, calibration,
sensor route, lifecycle, or stall response. Binary pass/fail identifies that
an obligation failed but may not distinguish these causes. Textual diagnostics
can help, but may repeat assumptions embedded in a hand-written test.
Measurement-grounded feedback instead records commanded value, observed
value, expected postcondition, residual, route, and evidence identity. The
coding agent can use this evidence within a restricted repair budget, after
which the candidate must pass the same candidate-independent validator.

Whether measurement-grounded feedback improves repair is an empirical
question, as is whether the validator detects the relevant fault classes. The
thesis therefore treats repair as one stage in the lifecycle and evaluates it
against conditions such as no repair, verdict-only feedback, or less structured
diagnostics. Repair success is not inferred from the architecture alone.


\subsection[Comparative Synthesis]{Comparative Synthesis}
\label{sec:comparative-synthesis}

Table~\ref{tab:related-comparison} compares representative lines of work by

\section[Synthesis of the Research Gap]{Synthesis of the Research Gap}
\label{sec:research-gap}

The literature establishes four important facts. First, robot intelligence is
becoming more reusable across tasks and, to a growing extent, across
embodiments, but deployment still supplies an action-realisation interface.
Second, middleware and standard contracts improve reuse while leaving a
hardware- and runtime-specific component to the integrator. Third, program,
tool, skill, and driver synthesis show that executable artefacts can be
generated from specifications and context. Fourth, robot testing and runtime
verification show that robot-facing software must be judged by external
properties and observations rather than by source plausibility or self-report.

What remains underexplored is the intersection of these findings. For an integration instance---whether it involves a new robot or an existing
platform being connected to a different runtime, contract, or downstream
stack---the reviewed strands do not yet jointly evaluate
a method that:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.45em]
  \item instantiates a shared, consumer-facing primitive contract from both a
  structural robot description and explicit runtime information;
  \item produces a reusable adapter rather than a task-specific policy or a
  schema-only wrapper;
  \item derives candidate-independent, route-aware behavioural obligations
  while measuring the robot-specific validation work that remains;
  \item uses observed hardware or physics-grounded simulator residuals to
  support bounded repair while retaining external pass/fail authority; and
  \item evaluates the resulting layer in terms of synthesis success,
  downstream reuse, remaining manual effort, and failure boundaries across
  morphology and runtime.
\end{enumerate}

The gap is therefore more precise than the statement that robot primitives are
hand-written. Some skills are learned, some trajectories are generated, and
some interfaces are derived automatically. The repeated bottleneck is that the
\emph{executable realisation and qualification of a stable primitive contract}
remain largely manual, demonstrated, or assumed to exist for each embodiment
and route, including repeated developer-specific integrations of established
robots.

Auto-Adapter investigates how far that bottleneck can be automated. The
initial work supplies simulator-side evidence that LLM agents can synthesise
reusable drivers from robot descriptions \citep{wang2026autoadapter}. The
thesis extends this foundation to explicit contracts and runtime profiles,
candidate-independent and potentially amortised qualification,
evidence-grounded repair, and a boundary-oriented evaluation of capability
reuse. In this framing,
VLA models, learned policies, and agentic robot systems are important examples
of the intelligence that may consume a generated adapter; the scientific
object remains the adapter lifecycle itself.
