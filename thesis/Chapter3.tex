\chapter[AutoAdapter-Bench]{AutoAdapter-Bench: LLMs for Robot Drivers}
\label{chapter:autoadapter-bench}

\begin{quote}\itshape
This chapter presents AutoAdapter-Bench, a benchmark for comparing how
different LLM backbones perform in robot-driver tasks. The benchmark separates
two abilities that are often conflated: synthesising robot-specific drivers and
using robot capability interfaces to complete tasks.
\end{quote}

\section[Introduction]{Introduction}

This chapter develops AutoAdapter-Bench to examine how LLM backbone choice
affects two roles in robot-driver systems: synthesising robot-specific drivers
and supporting high-level control through robot capability interfaces. In both
tracks, the surrounding architecture provides a common experimental setting,
while the LLM backbone remains the object of comparison. The chapter therefore
does not investigate how specific design choices in Auto-Adapter or other
driver-synthesis frameworks affect synthesis performance, nor does it compare
alternative high-level-control architectures.

The first track compares seven LLM backbones within the same Auto-Adapter
generation framework across 14 robot configurations. Generated drivers are
assessed against common executable validation criteria. The comparison considers
the proportion of drivers that pass validation, the cost of generation, and the
repair effort required to produce a valid submission. Together, these measures
show whether backbone choice changes the reliability and efficiency of driver
synthesis under a shared generation design.

The second track compares the same seven backbones within a Code-BT high-level
controller. For each robot, the driver, capability interface, controller
structure, and task conditions remain common across backbones. Performance is
measured primarily by complete-task success, while capability-selection and
execution failures help explain differences between models. This track therefore
evaluates how effectively each backbone operates through an existing robot
interface within the same control architecture.

Because the two tracks examine different model roles, their outcomes are
analysed separately rather than combined into a single backbone ranking. The
remainder of the chapter defines the benchmark and research questions, describes
the experimental setup, presents the results from both tracks, and discusses
their implications for LLM backbone selection.

\section[Background and Research Questions]{Background and Research Questions}

Code-generation benchmarks commonly evaluate whether model-produced programs
pass executable tests, whereas many LLM-based robot controllers assume that
actions or tools are already available and assess planning or task completion
\citep{chen2021codex,jimenez2024swebench,ahn2022saycan,
singh2023progprompt}. Neither setting alone reveals how the same LLM performs
when constructing a robot-specific driver and when operating through an existing
capability interface.

AutoAdapter-Bench therefore defines backbone capability as observed performance
under shared system conditions, rather than as an intrinsic property of a model.
B1 compares backbones within Auto-Adapter, while B2 compares them within Code-BT.
The chapter addresses two corresponding questions:

\paragraph{\textit{RQ1a---Driver synthesis.}}
Under a common Auto-Adapter generation framework and executable validation
standard, to what extent do LLM backbones differ in synthesising robot-specific
drivers that pass validation under the skeleton-assisted condition and the
prespecified from-scratch ablation?

\paragraph{\textit{RQ1b---Capability-interface use.}}
Under a common Code-BT architecture, with the validated driver, capability
interface, and matched tasks held constant, to what extent do LLM backbones
differ in complete-task success?

The findings are conditional on these benchmark settings. They do not compare
alternative synthesis frameworks or controller architectures, and do not
estimate morphology effects.

\subsection{Benchmark Design}

B1 compares LLM backbones for driver synthesis while holding the Auto-Adapter
framework and evaluation protocol constant. B2 compares the same backbones for
capability-interface use while holding Code-BT, the validated driver, and the
robot capability interface constant \citep{zhang2025codebt}. Because the tracks
use different experimental units and define success at different levels, their
results are reported separately and are not combined into an aggregate score.

\subsection{Backbone and Robot Cohorts}

The seven backbones form a purposive comparison cohort rather than a
representative sample of available LLMs. The selection extends the cross-model
design of AutoAdapter 1.0 \citep{wang2026autoadapter}. Three Anthropic models
provide a within-provider comparison across model tiers, while the remaining
models extend the study across four additional providers. Smaller open-weight
model families are included to examine whether successful driver synthesis and
interface use are confined to proprietary systems. The resulting comparisons
are conditional on these seven models and the benchmark conditions.

The B1 cohort contains 11 exact robot configurations spanning manipulation,
dexterous-hand, legged, humanoid, mobile, and bimanual platforms. The
skeleton-assisted condition covers the complete cohort. The five-robot
from-scratch subset was selected before outcomes were inspected to bound the
additional experiment while retaining contrasting integration settings. B2
uses SO-101 and Unitree Go2 to provide one manipulation and one locomotion
setting. Robot configuration is treated as a blocking factor rather than as
evidence of a morphology effect. Canonical configuration identifiers and
package versions are reported in
Appendix~\ref{tab:appendix-benchmark-scope}.

\subsection{Success Criteria and Validation}

Industrial robot performance standards separate measurement procedures from
application-specific acceptance limits. ISO~9283 defines performance criteria
and related test methods for manipulating industrial robots, including pose
accuracy, pose repeatability, path accuracy, and path repeatability
\citep{iso9283}. It does not prescribe one numerical pass threshold for every
robot and application. ISO~9946 instead standardises how manufacturers present
robot characteristics \citep{iso9946}. For context, Universal Robots reports
UR5e pose repeatability of \(\pm 0.03\,\mathrm{mm}\) under ISO~9283, whereas
Franka Robotics reports less than \(\pm 0.1\,\mathrm{mm}\) for Franka
Research~3 under specified workspace and pose conditions
\citep{universalrobots2026ur5e,franka2026research3}. These figures are
manufacturer-reported repeatability specifications, not acceptance thresholds
adopted by this thesis. Repeatability describes the dispersion among repeated
visits to the same commanded pose and is distinct from absolute accuracy
\citep{messina2019target}. A single terminal endpoint error measured in
Direct MuJoCo is therefore neither an ISO~9283 repeatability measurement nor
evidence of industrial positioning accuracy.

The reach-success criterion used in this study comes from public
reinforcement-learning task definitions. At the pinned Meta-World commit,
Sawyer Reach v3 uses \(0.05\,\mathrm{m}\) as the Euclidean-distance
threshold between the tool centre point and its target
\citep{farama2026metaworldreach}. Farama FetchReach likewise uses
\(0.05\,\mathrm{m}\) as the Euclidean-distance threshold between the end
effector and its goal \citep{farama2026fetchreach}. Meta-World includes the
exact boundary, whereas the FetchReach documentation uses a strict
inequality.

This thesis therefore interprets \(0.05\,\mathrm{m}\) as a source-benchmark
task-completion convention, not as an industrial positioning tolerance. A
position-only binary result does not establish end-effector orientation,
stable dwell, collision-free execution, contact validity, or physical safety;
these properties require separate criteria and trusted guards. Reference-driver
endpoint errors are retained only as positive-control execution results. They
test whether the simulator, measurement binding, trusted Harness, and
reference controller can execute the declared task under the evaluated
conditions. They are not used to select, tune, or justify the acceptance
threshold, because deriving a threshold from those outcomes would make the
requirement depend on the calibration controller used to exercise it.
